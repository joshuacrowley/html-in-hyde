# LinkedIn article draft — v2

**Suggested title:** I Went to the Park and Wrote HTML by Hand

**Suggested subtitle (if LinkedIn gives you the field):** HTML in Hyde #2, an afternoon without agents, and a question I'm still sitting with

---

On Saturday I took my laptop to Hyde Park in Sydney and wrote HTML by hand.

The event was HTML in Hyde #2, organised by Max Bo for HTML Day 2026. Picnic blankets on the grass near the Anzac Memorial, laptops everywhere, everyone tethering off phone hotspots. And the brief was about as simple as a brief gets: sit down with other people and write some HTML. That's it. Less a meetup, more a freewrite — nobody talking much about the web, everybody quietly making a little bit of it.

Now, I write a lot of HTML. It's kind of my whole thing. But these days it's almost always with AI agents in the loop — I'm describing, the agent is typing, and the tags appear. So I'll be honest: sitting down to hand-code again felt a bit daunting. I hadn't done it properly in a while, and I genuinely wasn't sure how much was still in my hands.

## What actually happened

More than I expected, is the answer. I was surprised how much I remembered. I was equally surprised by how many old crutches I reached for. Stack Overflow. W3Schools. Chrome DevTools, nudging properties around until something looked right. At one point I wrote a `console.log` — which I haven't done in ages. Hello, old friend.

And here's the thing I kept noticing while I sat there making mistakes: HTML was designed to be handwritten. You can typo a tag, forget to close something, and the browser doesn't throw its hands up — the document still does its best to render. There aren't many formats that generous. It's a very human material, and you feel that most when you're touching it directly.

By the end of the afternoon we had a gallery. Games, little works of art, a hypothetical MySpace profile. Nothing polished — just what a bunch of people can make in a few hours with HTML, CSS, and a laptop on the grass. I had a blast.

## The thing I'm still chewing on

The picnic was the easy part. The question it left me with is bigger.

As we hand more of the implementation to agents, a quiet gap opens up. The work still ships, the pages still render — but day to day, you can drift away from the basic materials. The tags, the cascade, the browser doing its generous best with imperfect markup. You stop touching the stuff.

And that matters, because HTML isn't just something we consume. It got to where it is through builders around the world arguing about specs and requirements — consensus, hashed out over years, lots of tiny decisions about how documents should behave. The material has a history of people being close enough to it to push back on it.

So if most implementation happens through agents, how do you stay connected to the material? If the people shaping products are a layer removed from HTML, CSS, and the browser, where do taste and frustration and craft feed back into how the platform evolves?

I don't have a neat answer. It's a bit like collaboration with AI — I've said before I'm not sure what good looks like there yet, and this feels like a cousin of that question. But I think the drift is real, and I think it'll shape more than our workflows.

To be clear, this isn't a "real developers write every tag by hand" thing. I'm not anti-agent — I'll be back working with them on Monday, happily. It's practical. It's the same reason I keep banging on about capturing ideas as single-file HTML prototypes: keeping a working relationship with the material is what lets you cut through the hype and actually feel what the web can do.

An afternoon in the park turns out to be a pretty good way to do that. You feel the resistance again. You remember the material is still there, still forgiving, waiting to be touched.

If an HTML Day thing pops up near you, give it a crack. Or just open a blank file and start typing tags. The browser will meet you halfway — it always has.
